Climate decisions made far from Antarctica depend on measurements made there.
Ice, ocean, atmosphere, and ecosystems must be observed consistently before
their changes can be detected, explained, projected, or acted upon.

## Proposition

**Sustained observation of Antarctica and the Southern Ocean should be treated
as global public infrastructure.** Governments, national Antarctic programs,
research funders, and international scientific bodies should plan for the
continuity, coordination, and public usefulness of those observations—not only
for the completion of individual projects.

“Public infrastructure” describes a function, not an owner. Antarctic stations,
satellites, autonomous instruments, research vessels, data centers, and the
people who operate them may belong to different nations and institutions. The
measurements form a shared system whose value crosses borders.

## Supporting evidence

The [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/media/update/scientists-and-experts-gather-united-kingdom-shape-future-of-global-climate-observation)
describes the global climate observing system—from satellites to Antarctic
research stations—as a shared public good. It identifies long-term reliability,
coordination, secure data streams, storage, sharing, and accessibility as parts
of the same problem.

The [Southern Ocean Observing System](https://www.soos.aq/about-us)
exists to facilitate the sustained collection and delivery of essential
observations of Southern Ocean change. Its mission reflects a practical truth:
no single expedition, institution, or country can observe the system adequately
on its own.

The legal and scientific culture of Antarctica already recognizes the public
value of knowledge. Article III of the [Antarctic
Treaty](https://www.ats.aq/e/antarctictreaty.html) provides that scientific
observations and results from Antarctica shall be exchanged and made freely
available. The [Scientific Committee on Antarctic Research's data
policy](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7825314)
extends that principle into modern expectations for preservation,
accessibility, interoperability, and responsible stewardship.

Continuity is especially important. A missed direct observation usually cannot
be recreated later. Gaps can sometimes be estimated from neighboring data or
models, but those estimates are not the measurements that would have been made.
A break in a long record may impair the ability to distinguish a short-lived
event from a persistent change, evaluate a model, or recognize a threshold
while it is being crossed.

## Recommended action

Krigsvold therefore supports five practical commitments:

1. **Protect long-term records.** Fund essential observing programs on
   multi-year horizons and require continuity plans for measurements whose
   scientific value depends on an unbroken time series.
2. **Publish risks to continuity.** National programs and funders should identify
   vulnerable instruments, missions, data services, and records before a loss
   becomes irreversible.
3. **Coordinate internationally.** New investments should fill documented gaps,
   use compatible standards, and strengthen the observing system as a whole.
4. **Preserve and share the data.** Publicly funded observations should be
   accompanied by durable archives, usable metadata, documented methods, and
   timely access.
5. **Fund the full chain.** Instruments without calibration, maintenance,
   transmission, quality control, stewardship, and skilled personnel do not
   constitute a reliable observing system.

## Limits and tradeoffs

Treating observation as infrastructure does not mean preserving every project
or instrument indefinitely. Programs should still be evaluated for scientific
value, duplication, cost, safety, reliability, and environmental impact.
Priorities will be necessary.

Nor is this an argument for an unlimited permanent footprint in Antarctica.
Satellites, autonomous platforms, shared logistics, and carefully designed
remote systems may sometimes provide better coverage with less risk and less
disturbance. In other cases, direct field observation is irreplaceable. The
choice should follow the measurement need.

Open access also requires disciplined stewardship. Quality control, complete
metadata, durable repositories, and clear provenance take time and money. Those
functions are part of the infrastructure rather than administrative residue to
be funded only if a project has money left over.

Finally, observation does not itself decide policy. It narrows uncertainty,
tests explanations, and makes consequences more visible. Governments and
communities must still make judgments about risk, cost, responsibility, and
action.
